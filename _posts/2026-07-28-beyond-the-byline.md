A publication is one of science’s most durable records. It preserves a finding, the argument around it, and the names of the people who produced it. But a byline is intentionally compact. It rarely tells us who designed an experiment, adapted a difficult method, wrote the analysis software, or made the judgment that changed the direction of the work.

That missing detail matters. Scientific careers are built from specific contributions, yet the signals used to understand those careers are often broad: job titles, publication counts, author order, and lists of skills without context. These signals are useful, but they cannot fully describe what a person actually did.

## A more specific record

Benchpro is being built around a simple idea: scientific capability is easier to understand when it stays connected to the work where it was demonstrated.

A useful contribution record should answer a small set of practical questions:

- **What capability was used?** Name the method, analysis, software, or technical practice precisely.
- **What did the person contribute?** Distinguish observation, direct participation, and leadership without turning them into a universal ranking.
- **Where did the work happen?** Connect the contribution to a publication or another clear research context when available.
- **Who can speak to it?** Keep peer review attached to the specific claim it addresses.

The boundaries are as important as the record itself. A linked publication provides context; it does not prove every individual contribution. A self-reported role describes a claim; it is not a certification. Peer confirmation can support that claim; it does not make a judgment about everything a scientist knows or may do next.

## Why evidence and context belong together

A keyword such as “single-cell RNA sequencing” can mean very different things. Someone may have observed the workflow, prepared samples, run the instrument, designed the experiment, built the quality-control process, or led the analysis. Each is meaningful, but they are not interchangeable.

Context makes the capability legible. It gives collaborators, hiring teams, and other scientists a better starting point for a real conversation. It also gives the person who did the work a more durable form of recognition—one that can travel beyond a paper, laboratory, institution, or job title.

Benchpro’s aim is not to replace publications, CVs, or professional judgment. It is to add a missing layer: a person-specific account of contribution that remains close to its source and transparent about its review state.
